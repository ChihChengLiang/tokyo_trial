Who ran wartime Japan

Under the Meiji Constitution the cabinet did not command the army or navy. The two General Staffs answered directly to the emperor. Pick a cabinet to see who held each post. A red square marks someone later tried at Tokyo; tap any underlined name.

Tried at TokyoDates in grey show when someone joined or left a post mid-cabinet.
Right of supreme commandMilitary operations were the emperor's prerogative, exercised through the Army and Navy General Staffs. Civilian ministers had no formal say over them, which let field armies like the Kwantung Army act first and inform Tokyo later.
Active-duty minister ruleFrom 1936 the War and Navy Ministers had to be serving officers. By refusing to supply one, the army could block or bring down a cabinet, as it did to Yonai in 1940.
Collective decisions, blurred blameWar policy was agreed at Liaison Conferences and ratified at Imperial Conferences. This consensus system made it hard for the tribunal to pin decisions on individuals, and it made the conspiracy charge a stretch.

The process, step by step

    What they were charged with

    The indictment's 55 counts fell into three groups. The court threw out most as duplicates or outside its Charter and ruled on only ten.

    Counts 1–36

    Crimes against peace

    Conspiring to wage, planning and waging aggressive war. The court kept Count 1 (overall conspiracy) and the counts for actually waging war on each country: 27, 29, 31, 32, 33, 35, 36. Only this category put someone in the "Class A" dock.

    Counts 37–52

    Murder

    Charged killings in unlawful war as murder, for example at Pearl Harbor or Nanjing. The court declined to rule on any of them, holding they were covered by other counts or not provided for in the Charter.

    Counts 53–55

    War crimes & crimes against humanity

    The court ruled on Count 54 (ordering, authorizing or permitting atrocities) and Count 55 (recklessly failing to prevent them), the command-responsibility count.

    What stayed out of the courtroom

    The emperor

    Hirohito was not indicted or called as a witness. U.S. officials and defendants worked to keep blame away from him. Webb and Bernard both raised his absence.

    Unit 731 and germ warfare

    Shiro Ishii's men got U.S. immunity for their experimental data. Röling later called learning of this "a bitter experience".

    Crimes against colonial subjects

    Forced labor and the "comfort women" system in Korea and Taiwan went largely unaddressed. Several of the judging powers were colonial empires themselves.

    Bombing of cities, on both sides

    Japan's air raids on Chinese cities, above all the years-long bombing of Chongqing (1938–43), were never charged. The Allies had firebombed Tokyo and dropped two atomic bombs, and prosecuting air raids would have invited the same charge against them. There was also no clear treaty law on aerial bombing. Pal and defense counsel like Blakeney pressed the point anyway.

    Many other suspects

    Dozens of Class A suspects, including future Prime Minister Kishi, were freed without trial in 1947–48 as the Cold War changed U.S. priorities.
